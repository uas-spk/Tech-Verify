45% was the requirement. We delivered 61.63% — and that number is why the busbar was accepted.

14 September 2026 · Umer Siddiqui

An EPC ordered 6000 A aluminium busbar trunking with one unusual condition written into the award: the conductor had to demonstrate a minimum conductivity, expressed as a percentage of the International Annealed Copper Standard. Not "aluminium busbar to standard". A number, verified, before acceptance.

That condition exists because aluminium is not one material. EC-grade 1350 alloy runs around 61% IACS; common structural extrusion alloys in the 6000 series land in the low-to-mid fifties, and an off-spec or recycled billet can fall further. The bar looks identical in all three cases. Painted, bolted and enclosed, nobody on site can tell them apart — but the temperature rise, the losses and the long-term joint behaviour are decided at the billet, months before anyone energizes anything.

Two busbars can carry the same nameplate rating and be separated by 15% conductivity. One passes the type test. The other cooks.

So the sequence was: specify the alloy and temper, confirm the mill certificate, inspect the extrusion plant, and test the delivered conductor — ASTM B193 resistivity, converted to % IACS — before the assembly went to its IEC 61439 verification. The result came back at 61.63% against a 45% requirement. Comfortably clear, on paper, with the report attached to the acceptance file.

Three things that number bought, in order of how much they mattered:

  • Acceptance without argument. The condition was met in evidence, not in assurance. No site debate, no conditional sign-off, no retention held back pending "clarification".
  • A defensible temperature-rise case. Conductivity feeds directly into I²R losses, which feed the temperature-rise verification. Meeting the conductivity floor with margin is what makes the thermal result reproducible rather than lucky.
  • A file that survives the bad day. If a joint ever runs hot in service, the question becomes "was the conductor compliant?" With a dated third-party number on record, that question closes in one email instead of one investigation.

The wider point has nothing to do with busbar. When a buyer writes a number into a specification, they are telling you exactly where they have been burned before. Treat it as the acceptance gate it is: verify it upstream, in the factory, on the material — not downstream, on site, on the schedule.

Takeaway: a specified number is not a target to approach — it is a gate to clear with margin, evidenced before shipment. Conductivity, temperature rise, ingress rating, refrigerant type: the cheapest place to prove any of them is the factory floor.

Five motors, one week in Germany: what a witnessed FAT actually catches

12 September 2026 · Umer Siddiqui

Three high-voltage and two medium-voltage machines, tested at the manufacturer's works before release. Heat runs, no-load and locked-rotor characteristics, vibration, insulation, and the paperwork that has to travel with each serial number. A week of standing on a test floor — and a week that is routinely the first thing cut when a schedule slips.

Here is what witnessing catches that a stamped certificate never will:

  • The machine tested is the machine shipped. Serial numbers and nameplates are checked against the test report in front of you. A type-test certificate for a family member is not a routine test for your unit.
  • Deviation handling in real time. A vibration reading slightly outside the ISO band, a temperature-rise class judged by extrapolation, a bearing re-greased "just before" the run. On the floor, these get explained, repeated or corrected. In a report, they get rounded.
  • Accessories that quietly differ. Winding and bearing RTDs, space heaters, terminal box orientation, cable-entry size, mounting and shaft details — the bits that do not stop a motor from spinning on a test bed but do stop it from being installable.
  • Hazardous-area detail. Where the machine is Ex-rated, the marking string, gas group, temperature class and certificate scope must match the zone it is going to. A mismatch here is not a paperwork issue; it is an ignition source.
  • What "burn-in" is really for. Electronics and control assemblies fail early or fail late, rarely in between. Running them hot and powered for an extended, documented period is how infant mortality surfaces in a factory rather than in a live process.

A failure found on the test floor costs a technician and a day. The same failure found at commissioning costs a mobilized crew, a delayed handover, and your place on the next bid list.

And this is the part buyers underestimate: the value is not only technical. A witnessed test creates a dated, independent baseline. Months later, when something misbehaves in service, that baseline is what turns a blame contest — site conditions versus product defect — into a ten-minute comparison. Without it, every argument is opinion against opinion, and the supplier usually loses on reputation even when they are right on engineering.

Takeaway: the test week is not a formality bolted to the end of manufacturing. It is the cheapest insurance the product line will ever buy — and the only version of the truth that both parties will accept later.
