CE or UKCA: which mark does your product actually need now?

Since Brexit, Great Britain runs its own conformity mark, UKCA, alongside the EU's CE. The two are separate systems with largely mirrored technical requirements, and the practical question for most manufacturers is not which is stricter but which route the buyer will accept.

The short version

  • Selling into the EU: CE marking, based on the applicable EU directives or regulations for your product.
  • Selling into Great Britain: UKCA is the domestic mark. For a large group of product regulations the UK government has extended recognition of CE marking indefinitely, so CE-marked goods can still be placed on the GB market.
  • Northern Ireland follows the EU rules, with the UKNI marking used in specific cases where a UK body has done the conformity assessment.

Where people get caught

The extension of CE recognition is not universal. It covers a defined list of regulations, and some sectors — construction products and medical devices being the usual examples — follow their own timelines. Before you decide to skip UKCA, confirm the position for your specific regulation rather than for "the UK" in general.

The second trap is documentation rather than marking. Whichever mark you apply, you still need a technical file, a declaration of conformity naming the right legislation, and a responsible person or importer established in the relevant market. A correct mark on a product with a thin technical file fails the first serious audit.

Worth checking: scopes and deadlines here have moved several times and can change again. Verify the current position on the UK government and EU Commission pages for your product's regulation before committing to a route.

Saudi Arabia: how SABER really works, step by step

Nearly every product shipped into Saudi Arabia passes through SABER, the online conformity platform operated by SASO. Importers who treat it as a customs formality discover at the port that it is a two-stage certification process, and that the first stage should have started weeks earlier.

The two certificates

  1. Product Certificate of Conformity (PCoC) — issued once per product model against the applicable Saudi technical regulation, by a conformity assessment body approved on the platform. Valid for a year, and this is the stage that needs test reports.
  2. Shipment Certificate of Conformity (SCoC) — issued per consignment, referencing the PCoC and the shipping documents. Without it, the shipment does not clear.

What slows it down

  • Test reports that do not match the regulation cited on the platform, or come from a laboratory whose scope does not cover that standard.
  • Product classification — the HS code and category drive which technical regulation applies. Get this wrong and you certify against the wrong requirements.
  • Energy-efficiency and labelling requirements for appliances and lighting, which sit on top of the safety regulation.
  • Arabic labelling and marking details, which are checked in practice and not only on paper.

Plan the product certificate around the lead time of whichever test is slowest, not around the shipping date. For electrical goods that is usually safety and EMC testing; for building products it is often a physical performance test that cannot be rushed.

Worth checking: the list of regulated products, approved bodies and fees on SABER is updated regularly. Confirm your product's current category on the platform before booking tests.

How to tell whether a test lab is the right one

Two laboratories can quote the same test at the same price, and only one of the reports will be accepted by your customer. The difference is rarely the equipment. It is accreditation, scope, and whether the report says what the buyer's specification asks for.

Four things to confirm before you book

  1. Accreditation to ISO/IEC 17025. This is the competence standard for testing and calibration laboratories. Ask for the certificate, and note the accreditation body.
  2. The scope, not just the certificate. Accreditation is granted for specific tests and standards. A laboratory can be genuinely accredited and still be outside scope for the clause you need — ask them to point to your standard in their scope document.
  3. The right kind of mark for the destination. For equipment used in US workplaces, buyers often require certification by an OSHA-recognised NRTL; in Canada, a body accredited by the Standards Council of Canada. A test report is not the same thing as product certification.
  4. What the report will contain. Ask for a sample report for a similar product, and check it states the standard edition, sample identification, conditions and a clear result.

On failed tests

A failure found in a laboratory is cheap; the same failure found by a customer is not. When a test fails, get the measured values and the clause that was not met, fix the design against that clause, and retest only the affected part where the standard allows it. Ask about partial retest cost before you start — it varies widely and it is negotiable.

Worth checking: if your buyer names a specific certification body or mark in their specification, that requirement overrides any equivalent you consider technically sufficient.
