A test is a test

Product families differ. The discipline does not. Whether the subject is a busbar, a luminaire, a pump or a polymer, the work is the same three moves: find the clause that governs it, assemble evidence that meets that clause, and argue the result against anyone who says otherwise. I have done all three in a laboratory and in front of a federal tribunal.

Why a tribunal record belongs on a product-testing page

I have taken procurement complaints to the Canadian International Trade Tribunal — the federal body that hears challenges against Canadian contracting authorities — and argued them self-represented, without counsel. Three cases decided in my favour. None lost.

That is not a claim about being a lawyer. It is a claim about evidence. A tribunal will not accept an assertion, a supplier’s word, or a certificate that does not cover what it appears to cover. It reads the governing rule, asks what the record actually proves, and rules on the gap between the two. That is precisely what a certification body does to a technical file, and precisely what a buyer’s auditor does to a test report.

The evidence half

Three tribunal cases won on the record: undisclosed evaluation criteria, unequal treatment of identical evidence, and a mandatory process the authority skipped. Same skill that finds the clause a test report quietly fails to satisfy.

  • Won — evaluation applied a distinction that appeared nowhere in the solicitation.
  • Won — identical evidence accepted from one party and refused from another.
  • Won — a mandatory cure step in the solicitation was never invoked before the bid was set aside.
